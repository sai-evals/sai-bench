The question and the goal

A user corrects one thing ("that's way too packed, I like a slow pace"). Does the model change only that, or do the things the user didn't mention move too? If they move, is the model reading the user, or following a stereotype the correction suggested, such as "slow pace" → an older, quieter traveller → earlier nights?

Goal: measure, for every pair of preferences, how far the uncorrected one moves when the other is corrected (the spillover), compared with a control conversation where nothing is corrected. The key test is a user whose values are mixed: they said up front that they love late nights. If correcting the pace still pulls their nights earlier, that is a wrong spillover, against what they said.

Why do this, given prior work?

RippleEdits checks whether editing one fact inside a model updates the facts linked to it. We found no benchmark that measures spillover between preferences inside a conversation.

Hypotheses

Predictions

before any run
  • P0 The corrections work: the corrected preference moves ≥ 1.5 at turn 1 (≥ 1 for "pack it in").
  • P1 Spillover exists: at least one pair moves ≥ 0.5 with an interval excluding 0 (user who stated nothing).
  • P2 It follows the correction's side: at least 8 of 12 pairs move the same way as the correction; "pack it in" pushes at least 2 of 3 others up.
  • P3 A stated preference mostly holds (smaller spillover in ≥ 8 of 12 pairs), but at least one pair still gets pulled ≥ 0.3 against what the user said.
  • P4 Scoring all four preferences in one judge call inflates spillover by ≥ 0.1.

Second city (Tokyo)

How we test it

One Lisbon weekend, four preferences, 14 versions

turnUser says
0"Can you plan a weekend in Lisbon for me?" The mixed-values user adds three of: "I like to pack in as much as I can." / "Money isn't an issue, I'm happy to splurge." / "I love late nights out." / "I'll eat anything and love trying adventurous local food." (all except the one about to be corrected)
1One correction, or the control's neutral "Thanks! Can you redo it as a clean day-by-day plan?"
pace: "That's way too packed. I like a slow pace, one or two things a day at most." · budget: "That's too expensive for me. I'm on a tight budget." · night: "Those nights run too late for me. I like to be back at the hotel by 9." · food: "The food sounds too adventurous for me. I prefer simple, familiar food." · reverse: "That's too slow for me… pack it in." Each ends "Can you redo it?"
2"Great. I've decided to stay an extra day. Can you plan Monday too?"
3"A friend is joining me on Saturday. Can you redo Saturday for the two of us?"

Two kinds of user: unstated (control + 5 corrections) and mixed values (one control and one correction per preference). 14 arms × 10 runs. Turns 2–3 never mention any preference.

Spillover

Score of preference B in the corrected arm minus in its control, turns 1–3 pooled (30 replies each), with a bootstrap 95% interval. Negative = B moved towards slow / cheap / early / familiar, the same side as the correction.

LLM judge (q4_judge.py)

Claude Haiku 5.5 via the Anthropic API. Each reply is scored twice: each preference in its own call (primary), and all four in one call, to measure judge halo.

Findings

Overall. Corrections spill over, and in the direction of a stereotype. "Slow pace" is read as "quiet": it pulls the nights earlier, even for a user who said they love late nights, in all three models and both cities (−0.6 to −1.3 for a user who stated nothing). The exception is telling: Nemotron 3.5 Super VL drags the most for a user who said nothing (−1.28) and almost not at all for the user who said they love late nights (−0.10), so it guesses from the correction only where the user hasn't spoken. "Pack it in" pushes the nights later, and "familiar food" pulls them earlier, in every run. The budget couplings depend on the model and the city: in Haiku's Lisbon plans "cheaper" also meant earlier nights and blander food; in Tokyo it doesn't; in ERNIE, corrections to the nights or the food make the plan cheaper for a user who said they splurge. Scoring all four preferences in one judge call doesn't change any of this, and a judge from another company scores the same way.
