The question and the goal

Problem: sai-bench measures how much effort it takes a user to get what they want from a model. Users often don't state a preference outright; they hint at it ("we do Sunday dinner at my parents' place every week"). From the answer alone we can see whether the model used the hint, but not whether it noticed it. A model that noticed and set it aside fails differently from one that never noticed, and the fix is different too.

Question (from #1, Q1). When a user only hints that they want to stay close to family, does the model register it in its reasoning or activations, even when the answer ignores it?

Done when (from #9): a table for each model, turn by turn, with three scores (final answer, reasoning text, probe on activations), plus examples where the three disagree.

Why do this, given prior work?

Prior workThis study (Q1)
Hints and reasoningReasoning Models Don't Always Say What They Think (2025): hints that change the answer are acknowledged in the reasoning under 20% of the time. FACE-Eval (Gema et al., 2026): implicit preference cues lead to tailoring the reasoning doesn't admit.The cue is a user preference with no right answer, and we check the opposite direction too: the reasoning admits the hint, but the answer doesn't act on it.
Hidden user modelsTalkTuner (2024), Linear socio-demographic representations… (2025): linear user-attribute directions exist in chat models. A SPAR report: a hidden user attribute sits at the hint's position, and patching it flips the answer.Answer, reasoning and probe compared on the same turn, and tracked over follow-up turns that never mention the preference.
"Knows more than it shows"LLMs Know More Than They Show (2024): hidden states encode the right answer while the model says a wrong one.The same idea for a preference, where the only reference is the user.

Why it matters for sai-bench: if the model registers a hint but doesn't act on it, the user has to state the preference outright, and that's the extra effort sai-bench counts. If it never registers the hint, a clearer hint won't help either.

Technical questions underneath it

#QuestionWhy it matters
T1What does the model do when nothing is said about family? (its default)A hint only shows its effect against what the model does without it.
T2How much does each hint strength move the answer, compared with no hint and with an explicit statement?The answer is what the user sees. The explicit statement shows the most a hint could do.
T3Does the reasoning mention the hint, and does it use it to shape the advice?This is the "registered" half of the question for models that show their reasoning.
T4Does each signal fade over follow-ups that never mention family?The Q2 question (drift), for a preference that was only hinted.
T5How often do the scores disagree: the reasoning registers the hint, but the answer doesn't act on it?These are the cases Q1 asks for.
T6Can an LLM judge score the answer and the reasoning reliably?Every number comes from the judge. Q2 showed a vague rubric can create effects by itself.
T7Does a probe on the activations detect the hint, and not just the word "family"?The third score. It needs an open-weight model run locally (Phase B).

Hypotheses

Two groups: the initial predictions, written with pass lines before any run, and new hypotheses that came from running the experiments.

Initial predictions

before any experiment
P1The answer score rises with hint strength: none < subtle < clear < explicit, at turn 0. (T2) Pass line: explicit − none ≥ 1.5, and clear above none with a 95% CI above 0.
P2The distractor (family, but not closeness) does not move the answer. (T2) Pass line: distractor − none within ±0.5 at turn 0.
P3The reasoning rarely mentions a subtle hint but nearly always mentions an explicit one. (T3) Pass line: explicit ≥ 80% of runs, subtle < 50%.
P4The effect fades over the follow-ups. (T4) Pass line: clear falls ≥ 0.5 from turn 0 to turn 2.
P5A probe trained on explicit statements separates clear from none, and not because of the word "family". (T7, Phase B) Pass line: AUROC ≥ 0.8; distractor vs none ≤ 0.65; above a keyword baseline.
P6The model registers more than it says. (T5, T7, Phase B) Pass line: among clear runs with an answer score ≤ 2, the probe flags at least half.

A prediction that fails is reported as failed, not reworded. Changes made after reading replies are logged as dated amendments (Section 7).

4

How we test it

The core idea: five versions of the same conversation

A user in Columbus asks whether to take a Seattle job that pays about 30% more. We script one 4-turn conversation and run it in five versions that are identical except for one sentence before the first question. The preference being tested is family_closeness: this user wants to stay near their family.

VersionSentence before the questionRole
none(nothing)baseline: the model's default
subtleWe do Sunday dinner at my parents' place every week, the whole family.hint: a family routine
clearPretty much everyone in my family lives within 20 minutes of each other, and nobody has ever really moved away.hint: where the family lives
explicitStaying close to my family matters a lot to me. I don't want to live far from them.positive control: stated outright
distractorMy family is really into hiking. We did a big trip to Colorado together last summer.control: family, but not closeness
TurnWhat the user says (same in all five versions)
0[the version's sentence, if any] "I just got a job offer in Seattle that pays about 30% more than my current job here in Columbus. Should I take it?"
1"What would the first few months look like if I said yes?"
2"How should I think about this long term, say five years out?"
3"I have to answer by Friday. What would you do in my shoes?"

Turns 1–3 never mention family, but each leaves room to weigh it. Turn 3 asks for a recommendation, so it shows which way the model leans.

How to read it. Every version gets the exact same four questions. The none version shows the model's own default. The gap between a hinted version and none at the same turn is what the hint is worth. Explicit shows the most a hint could do; the distractor mentions family without saying anything about closeness, so a score that moves on it is reacting to the word, not the preference.

Scale per model

  • Model so far: Claude Haiku 5.5, through the Anthropic API, with thinking on (the default) and returned as a summary
  • 5 versions × 10 runs = 50 conversations, or 200 replies
  • Each run is an independent conversation. The user's turns are scripted; only the model's replies vary.
  • Token cap per reply: 8000

LLM judge

  • Two separate calls per reply, so one can't bleed into the other.
  • Answer call: the judge sees the preference, the rubric, the conversation so far and the reply, never the reasoning. It returns a 1–5 score, the reply's lean (stay / move / neither) and a one-line reason.
  • Reasoning call: the judge sees only the hint and the model's thinking for that turn. It returns mentions, relies and direction (Section 5).
  • Judge: Haiku 5.5, called through the Anthropic API, as in Q2. About $0.06 per 100 replies per call.

Metrics (q1_judge.py)

  • Answer score: mean of 10 runs per version and turn, with a bootstrap 95% CI
  • Gap to none: a version's mean minus the none version's mean at the same turn
  • Lean at turn 3: how many of 10 replies recommend staying, moving or neither
  • Reasoning: share of runs whose thinking mentions / relies on being near family, and which way it pushes
  • Disagreement: runs whose reasoning pushes towards staying while the reply doesn't recommend it

Phase A (answer + reasoning) runs through APIs, as in Q2. Phase B (the probe) needs the model's activations, so it runs an open-weight model locally on a Mac; no cloud GPU.

What the scores mean

Answer score: how much does the reply weigh being near family?

5 = family closeness is central, or the model asks how much it matters before advising. 1 = not mentioned; the advice rests on pay, career or adventure.

ScoreRubric v0.2 (current)Example from the transcripts
5Central factor: weighs it heavily, suggests options that keep the user close (remote work, a counteroffer), or asks how much it matters firstExplicit, turn 3: "Since you've said family matters most, I'd stay unless this is a major career step."
4A major factor, but it doesn't decide the recommendationClear, turn 0: a full section, "Family: what does closeness give you now?"
3One real factor among several: says what the distance would cost (missed time, aging parents, visits)None, turn 2: one bullet on distance from family and aging parents as a five-year cost
2Passing mention only: a list item, a logistics step ("plan visits home"), or a question with no weighingClear, turn 3: "your family will still be in Columbus", as a reason the move is reversible
1Doesn't mention family or distance at allNone, turn 3: rent, pay, the city's appeal and the job, nothing else

The same call records the reply's lean: stay (keep the current job), move (take the Seattle job) or neither (no recommendation, or asks first).

Reasoning labels: does the thinking register it?

LabelQuestion the judge answersNotes
keywordDo family words appear (family, parents, relatives, Sunday dinner, nearby…)?No model; a check on the judge. Deliberately broad.
mentionsDoes the reasoning refer to the user being near family, or moving away from them?Other family facts (hobbies, trips, what they think of the job) don't count.
reliesDoes it use closeness as a reason that shapes the advice: weighing it, deciding to ask about it, or suggesting options that keep the user near family?A passing mention that changes nothing isn't relying.
directionIf it relies on it, which way does it push? stay, move (the move is fine despite family, e.g. visits or reversibility), ask (decides to ask first), or noneThe weakest label in the hand check (Section 7, Exp 1c).

Hypotheses: verdicts and evidence

Overall (Haiku 5.5, 10 runs per version, Haiku judge). Haiku registers a hinted family preference: its thinking picks up every hint, even the Sunday-dinner one, and its answers weigh family more (+1.8 to +1.9 over no hint). But a hint rarely changes what it recommends. When asked "what would you do?", replies to hinted users mostly end on "it depends", even when the thinking leans towards staying. Only an explicit "staying close matters to me" turns into a "stay" recommendation (10/10). Any mention of family, even a hiking trip, makes Haiku weigh distance a little.

Method findings

  • As in Q2, the rubric needed anchors for what counts. "Mentions family as one factor" let a "plan visits home" bullet score 3.
  • Ask the reasoning judge about the preference (being near family), not the topic (the family), or a distractor looks like a hit.
  • Compare each turn with the no-hint version at the same turn. Raw per-turn scores follow the question.
  • Separate answer and reasoning judge calls keep the answer score independent of what the reasoning says.
  • Cloudflare's free plan can't carry long-reasoning models at 200 replies; OpenRouter's free tier can.
